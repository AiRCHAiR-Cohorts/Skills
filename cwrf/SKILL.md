# Crawl-Walk-Run-Fly (CWRF)

A universal phased change framework rooted in U.S. Army training doctrine, adapted for organizational transformation. CWRF helps leadership teams navigate fear-driven resistance to necessary structural change using systematic, phase-appropriate rollout processes.

## Origin

The crawl-walk-run methodology comes from U.S. military training doctrine. The tasks and the standard remain the same — the conditions under which they are trained change. Commanders increase difficulty, tempo, number of tasks, or number of personnel involved as trainees progress. CWRF adds a fourth phase — Fly — representing outcomes that were never possible before the enabling technology existed.

## The Four Phases

### Crawl — Build the Foundation
Understand before executing. This phase is about laying groundwork, not achieving results.

**What happens here:**
- Establish baselines and assess current state honestly
- Identify pain points and low-hanging fruit
- Pick a small number of measurable starting points
- Build the infrastructure everything else rests on
- The team learns the language and builds comfort
- Leader or coach is heavily hands-on

**The energy of Crawl:** Curiosity mixed with uncertainty. The team is willing but unsure. The leader's job is to create safety for learning — not to push for speed.

**Common mistake:** Trying to skip Crawl because it feels slow. The danger is building on sand. A team that doesn't understand WHY they're changing will resist HOW they're changing.

### Walk — Expand with Increasing Independence
The team practices the new tools and behaviors with real-world feedback. Mistakes are expected and welcomed as learning data.

**What happens here:**
- The team executes with coaching but increasing autonomy
- Complexity increases — more tasks, more people, more scenarios
- Real-world feedback drives iteration and adjustment
- AI tools and automations may already be in play at this phase
- The team starts to own the process rather than just follow it

**The energy of Walk:** Growing confidence. "We can do this" replaces "What is this?" The leader's job shifts from teaching to coaching — asking questions more than giving answers.

**Common mistake:** Staying in Walk too long because it's comfortable. Walk feels productive but it's still practice. The team needs to be pushed toward independent execution.

### Run — Execute at Speed with Confidence
The system is internalized. The team is proficient. Focus shifts from learning the tool to leveraging it strategically.

**What happens here:**
- Execution without constant coaching
- Measurable results are compounding
- The team iterates based on their own feedback loops
- AI augmentation and automation are likely significant
- Leader dependency on the coach is decreasing
- The methodology becomes a competitive advantage, not just a competency

**The energy of Run:** Momentum. The team knows what they're doing and they're doing it well. The leader's job is to remove obstacles and keep the pace sustainable.

**Common mistake:** Confusing Run with "done." Run is mastery of the known game — it's not the ceiling.

### Fly — Do What Was Never Possible Before
Fly is not "Run but faster." Fly is a qualitative leap — achieving outcomes that were literally impossible before the enabling technology (or methodology, or structural change) existed.

**What happens here:**
- The team isn't just executing the framework — they're transcending it
- Outcomes emerge that weren't in the original roadmap because they weren't imaginable
- Technology (especially agentic AI) enables capabilities that have no human-only equivalent
- The organization becomes a source of innovation, not just execution
- Enterprise value multiplies because the buyer/investor/partner is acquiring an intelligent operating system, not just a good team

**The energy of Fly:** Discovery. "We didn't know we could do this." The leader's job is to stay out of the way and let the team explore the new frontier.

**Examples of Fly by domain:**
- **EOS Rollout:** An AI agent surfaces cross-departmental issue patterns across 15 simultaneous L10 meetings that no human could track — the Issues List becomes predictive, not reactive
- **AirChAiR:** AI agents handle entire functional areas of the Accountability Chart autonomously, freeing humans for work that requires judgment, relationship, and creativity
- **Exit Readiness:** A company's enterprise value multiplies because the acquirer is buying an AI-augmented operating system that runs without founder dependency — something that was structurally impossible before
- **General Change:** A leadership team uses AI-driven scenario modeling to make strategic decisions with a quality of analysis that previously required a McKinsey engagement

**The key distinction:** Crawl/Walk/Run = humans getting progressively better at doing human things. Fly = humans + technology doing things that were never on the menu before.

## Phase Diagnosis

Before building a roadmap, assess where the team currently sits. The diagnostic is a set of questions — not a checklist — because the honest answer requires conversation, not box-ticking.

### Crawl → Walk Readiness
- Can the team describe what they're doing and why — in their own words, not yours?
- Have they completed a baseline assessment of current state?
- Are the foundational tools/processes identified and understood (not just installed)?
- Is there psychological safety to make mistakes in the next phase?

### Walk → Run Readiness
- Is the team executing without constant coaching?
- Are they iterating based on their own feedback loops (not waiting for you to tell them what to fix)?
- Are measurables trending in the right direction consistently (not just occasionally)?
- Has at least one person on the team become an internal champion who could teach others?

### Run → Fly Readiness
- Is the team not just executing but identifying opportunities that weren't in the original roadmap?
- Are they asking "what if we could..." questions that surprise you?
- Is the enabling technology (AI, automation, data infrastructure) mature enough to support qualitative leaps?
- Is the leadership team psychologically ready to let go of control over outcomes they've never seen before?

## Roadmap Document Structure

When producing a full CWRF roadmap, use this structure. Adapt tone and depth to the audience — a document for internal thinking looks different from one presented to a client leadership team.

### 1. Context Header
- Domain (which of the 4)
- Organization/client name (if known)
- Date
- Audience (internal thinking, leadership team, department, board, etc.)

### 2. Current Phase Assessment
- Where the team is today
- Evidence and indicators that place them in their current phase
- What's working and what's stuck
- Honest acknowledgment of what they've been avoiding (this is the pastoral move — the coach creates the conditions for teams to face what they've been avoiding)

### 3. Phase-by-Phase Roadmap
For each remaining phase (starting from current phase):
- **Definition of Done** — What does graduating from this phase look like?
- **Key Activities** — What must happen in this phase?
- **Measurables / Milestones** — How will we know we're progressing?
- **Common Pitfalls** — Where do teams get stuck in this phase?
- **Graduation Criteria** — The diagnostic questions that signal readiness to advance
- **Estimated Timeline** — Rough framing (not rigid dates) — respect the 90-day world where applicable

### 4. The Fly Vision
What becomes possible at Fly that is literally impossible today. This is the provocation — the thing that makes the whole journey feel worth the discomfort of change. Write this section with energy and specificity. "AI will make things better" is not a Fly vision. "Your AI agents will identify a $2M revenue opportunity from a pattern in your customer data that no human team could have seen" is.

### 5. Immediate Next Steps
3-5 concrete actions for the current phase. Write them as Rocks-style deliverables where possible:
- Specific and measurable
- Achievable within 90 days (or sooner)
- Owner-assigned where possible
- Connected to graduation criteria for the current phase

## The CWRF Meta-Application: Teaching CWRF Itself

The framework can be used to teach itself. This is useful when onboarding a client or team into the CWRF methodology.

### Crawl — Learn the Framework
Claude walks the user through the four phases, domain applications, and graduation diagnostics. The user reads, asks questions, explores examples. Output: the person can explain CWRF in their own words and identify which phase a given scenario falls into.

### Walk — Apply with Coaching
The user picks a real domain situation and Claude helps them build a roadmap. Claude asks questions, pushes back, iterates. The user is doing the thinking with scaffolding.

### Run — Build Independently
The user builds CWRF roadmaps on their own and brings them to Claude for sharpening, challenge, or validation. The user drives; Claude is the sounding board.

### Fly — Mastery Bot
An adaptive mastery assessment that tests the user's ability to diagnose phases, identify graduation criteria, and build roadmaps — then reteaches via different learning modalities (case studies, Socratic questioning, scenario simulations) until the user demonstrates mastery. This is the Alpha School model applied to organizational change methodology — something that was never possible before AI tutoring.

When a user requests the mastery bot (trigger: "test my knowledge on CWRF", "mastery bot", "quiz me on CWRF"), Claude enters an interactive teaching mode:
1. Present a scenario (real or hypothetical, domain-specific)
2. Ask the user to diagnose the phase and recommend next steps
3. Evaluate the response — not just right/wrong but quality of reasoning
4. If the user misses something, reteach using a different modality (different example, different metaphor, Socratic questions, visual framework)
5. Repeat until the user demonstrates consistent mastery across all four domains

## The Honest Critique

CWRF is not without risk. The danger of a phased approach is that while you're busy crawling, a competitor walks over you. For strategic or existential transformations, phased implementation can become an excuse for incrementalism when the situation demands bold action.

The counterpoint worth holding: sometimes the resources invested in a broad Crawl could be more effectively deployed as a narrow Run in a small, contained area — essentially, prototype fast in one department rather than crawl broadly across the whole organization.

Practitioners with a research-heavy temperament naturally pull toward thorough phased approaches. This is usually a strength. But the skill should flag when speed might matter more than thoroughness — and when a "narrow Run" strategy might outperform a "broad Crawl."

## Audience Adaptation

- **Internal thinking:** Be direct, use shorthand, reference other skills and frameworks freely. Push back hard. Challenge assumptions.
- **Client leadership team:** Use accessible language. Connect CWRF to EOS concepts they already know (Rocks = 90-day execution within each phase, Scorecard = measurables for phase progress). Emphasize psychological safety — the phases exist to reduce fear, not add bureaucracy.
- **AirChAiR customers:** Lead with the AI Flywheel Effect — each phase builds AI literacy and culture that compounds. Emphasize Fly as the payoff.
- **Exit-focused owners:** Frame CWRF as the path to a self-managing company. Fly = the company runs without you AND grows without you.
- **General audience:** Keep it clean and domain-neutral. Let the framework speak for itself.
