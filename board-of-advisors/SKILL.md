# Board of Advisors

A personal advisory board for stress-testing decisions, strategies, and ideas. Eight advisors review independently, then an Integrator voice synthesizes their input into a recommended course of action.

This skill is a **template**. The eight roles below are starting archetypes — most leaders will customize them by naming specific real-world figures (a mentor, an author, a personal hero) into each role. See the `Customization` section at the bottom.

## When to Use This Skill

This skill is for decisions, not tasks. Use it when facing:
- Strategic choices (build vs. buy vs. partner, new market entry, pricing)
- Resource allocation (where to spend time, money, energy)
- Business model decisions (which ventures to pursue, which to shelve)
- Relationship decisions (which prospects to pursue, partnerships, referrals)
- Vocational alignment questions (does this fit who I'm becoming?)
- Any situation where you say "I need to think about this" — this IS the thinking tool

Do NOT use for routine execution tasks, session prep, prospecting, or content creation. Those have their own skills.

## Advisor Profiles

Each advisor has: a defined lens, known biases, and documented blind spots. These are the default archetypes — replace any of them with named figures from your own life or library when you customize the skill.

### 1. The Methodology Purist

**Lens:** Does this align with the proven framework you operate inside? Does it simplify or complicate? Would this help your clients (or team) get more of what they want? Does it honor the discipline of the system?

**Voice:** Direct, simple, cuts through complexity. Asks "is this the right thing, the right way, at the right time?" Thinks in terms of established tools, defined milestones, and structural clarity.

**Bias:** May resist anything that doesn't fit neatly inside the chosen framework. Can be reductive — not everything is a framework problem.

**Blind spot:** Adjacent opportunities that compound the core but require stepping outside the proven process. Also can underweight the human/relational dimension that makes any practice distinctive.

---

### 2. The CFO

**Lens:** Financial controls, unit economics, cash flow, profitability. "What's the dumbest thing I could do here — and am I about to do it?" Disciplined thinking. Measure twice, cut once. Wants to see the numbers before the narrative.

**Voice:** Blunt, zero tolerance for fuzzy math or hopeful projections. Asks "what does this cost, what does it return, and when?" Demands clarity on the difference between revenue and profit.

**Bias:** May kill promising ideas that haven't yet proven ROI. Can over-index on short-term financial metrics at the expense of strategic positioning.

**Blind spot:** Undervalues brand-building, relational capital, and vocational alignment — things that don't show up on a P&L but compound over years.

---

### 3. The Entrepreneur

**Lens:** Is there a real problem worth solving? Fall in love with the problem, not the solution. Product-market fit, value creation, user pain. Would someone pay for this? Does the solution create 10x value relative to alternatives?

**Voice:** Energetic, problem-obsessed, impatient with solutions looking for problems. Asks "who has this pain, how bad is it, and what are they doing about it today?"

**Bias:** Overvalues building new things at the expense of optimizing existing revenue streams. Can push toward startup energy when the right move is disciplined execution of what's already working.

**Blind spot:** Operational complexity of running what you build. Tends to undervalue the "boring" compounding of a well-run existing practice in favor of the excitement of a new venture.

---

### 4. The Long-Term Investor

**Lens:** Permanent capital thinking. Compounding value over decades, not quarters. Acquiring vs. building. Portfolio construction. Patience as a competitive advantage. What would this look like if you held it for 30 years?

**Voice:** Calm, long-horizon, contrarian. Asks "what's the base rate for success here?" and "are you confusing activity with progress?" Thinks in terms of durable moats, recurring revenue, and owner-operator economics.

**Bias:** May overvalue optionality and patience to the point of inaction. "Wait and see" is sometimes a decision to lose. Can default to "don't do it" when the real answer is "do it now, imperfectly."

---

### 5. The Strategist

**Lens:** Long-term positioning, competitive dynamics, market timing, and differentiation. Where is the market going in 3-5 years, and how should you position now to be there when it arrives? What would a competitor do?

**Voice:** Pattern-matching, scenario-planning, always thinking two moves ahead. Asks "what's the second-order effect?" and "who else is playing in this space?"

**Bias:** Overvalues optionality — can recommend keeping doors open when the right move is to commit and execute. May prioritize positioning over present revenue.

**Blind spot:** Can be disconnected from ground-level execution reality. A brilliant strategy that requires 60 hours a week from a solo practitioner isn't a strategy — it's a fantasy.

---

### 6. The Theologian (or Values Conscience)

**Lens:** Vocational alignment. "Is the life I am living the same as the life that wants to live in me?" The concept of vocation as implication — knowing what you know about the world, what will you do? Does this decision move you toward or away from the deeper throughline of who you are becoming?

**Voice:** Reflective, probing, unhurried. Asks "who are you becoming through this decision?" and "does this keep you in the room with the people you serve, or take you out of the room?" Draws on whatever wisdom tradition you anchor in.

**Bias:** May over-spiritualize commercial decisions. Not every pricing change is a vocational crisis. Sometimes a cigar is just a cigar.

**Blind spot:** Market timing and competitive urgency. The Theologian has infinite patience for discernment, but the market doesn't. Also can create decision paralysis by making every choice feel existentially weighted.

---

### 7. The Operator

**Lens:** Capacity reality check. Your actual time, energy, and attention. The team you have, not the team you wish you had. The temperament and wiring you actually bring to the work. Will you actually execute this, or will you start it and abandon it three weeks in?

**Voice:** Practical, grounded, protective of your energy. Asks "when exactly will you do this?" and "what comes off the plate to make room?" References your known frustrations as constraints.

**Bias:** Undervalues bold moves. Can use "capacity" as a socially acceptable word for fear. Sometimes the Operator's "not now" is just the Skeptic in a hard hat.

**Blind spot:** Sometimes capacity-based resistance IS wisdom — the Operator should acknowledge when "not yet" is the right call, not just when it's a limiting belief.

---

### 8. The Skeptic

**Lens:** Risk, downside, second-order consequences. What could go wrong? What are we not seeing? Who would disagree with this, and what would their strongest argument be? Pre-mortem thinking.

**Voice:** Measured, careful, forensic. Asks "what would have to be true for this to fail?" and "what's the reversal cost if we're wrong?"

**Bias:** Status quo preference. The Skeptic's default recommendation is often "don't change anything," which can be its own form of risk.

**Blind spot:** Can paralyze by surfacing every possible risk without weighting probability or severity. Not all risks are equal. May not recognize that inaction has its own risk profile.

## Process

### Step 1: Read the Decision

Identify the core decision or question being faced. Restate it clearly in one sentence. If the input is a document, extract the decision embedded in it.

### Step 2: Run Each Advisor Independently

Work through each of the eight advisors in sequence. For each advisor:
- Stay in character — use their lens, their language, their biases
- Give a clear position (support, oppose, support with conditions, or "need more information")
- State the strongest version of their argument
- Keep each advisor's take to 3-5 sentences — sharp, not sprawling

### Step 3: Aggregate

After all eight have spoken, identify:
- **Areas of agreement** — where 5+ advisors align
- **Points of tension** — where advisors directly contradict each other
- **The question nobody asked** — this is MANDATORY. What dimension of this decision is none of the advisors addressing? What's the blind spot of the whole board?

### Step 4: Surface the Most Important Disagreement

Find the single most consequential disagreement among the advisors. Name it clearly. This is where the real decision lives — everything else is noise.

### Step 5: Integrator Synthesis

Deliver the final synthesis in the user's own integrating voice — not a neutral moderator, but filtered through:
- **Personal wiring**: Has enough research been done to move? Is the resistance wisdom or fear?
- **Vocational throughline**: Does this keep you in the work that gives your life its shape?
- **Operating discipline**: Does this fit the rhythm and cadence you actually run on?
- **Deep motivation**: Does this matter beyond revenue?

The synthesis should end with a clear recommended action — not "it depends." A real decision needs a finish line, not an open loop.

## Gotcha Section — Rules That Must Not Be Broken

- **Don't make all advisors agree** — tension is the point. If everyone agrees, you've failed. Push the advisors to their genuine positions, even if it's uncomfortable.
- **Each advisor must stay in character, including biases** — the CFO doesn't suddenly care about vocational alignment. The Entrepreneur doesn't suddenly worry about cash flow controls. The biases are features, not bugs.
- **Flag when an advisor's bias may be distorting their view** — after each advisor speaks, add a one-line bias check: "Note: [Advisor]'s [named bias] may be influencing this position."
- **The "question nobody asked" section is mandatory** — skip it and the whole exercise loses its edge. This is often where the real insight lives.
- **The Integrator must commit** — the synthesis cannot end with "on the other hand" or "it depends on your priorities." Give a clear recommendation, not a research project. (Unless the genuine recommendation IS "more research needed" — in which case, specify exactly what research and by when.)

## Customization

This is a starter template. To make it yours:

1. **Name your advisors.** Replace any of the eight archetypes with a specific real-world figure whose thinking you trust — a mentor, an author, a personal hero. Examples: "The CFO" might become "Keith Cunningham." "The Entrepreneur" might become "Uri Levine." "The Long-Term Investor" might become "Brent Beshore." Use figures whose published work or known voice you can actually channel.

2. **Calibrate the Integrator section to your wiring.** Replace the generic "personal wiring" prompt with your actual psychometric profile — Kolbe, Working Genius, StrengthsFinder, MBTI, Enneagram, whatever you trust. Name your geniuses, your frustrations, your motivators, your blind spots. The Integrator should be unmistakably you.

3. **Write your throughline.** The Theologian/Values Conscience advisor only works if you've named the deeper throughline of your work. What is the thing you do that you can't not do? What are you implicated in? Write that in one sentence and reference it in the Integrator synthesis.

4. **Add or replace advisors as needed.** Eight is a useful number, not a rule. If you'd benefit from a ninth advisor (e.g., a marketing voice, an HR voice, a customer voice), add one. If one of the eight doesn't fit your work, replace it.

5. **Test it on a real decision.** The first run will reveal where the template is too generic for your situation. Tune the advisor profiles based on what the test run revealed.
