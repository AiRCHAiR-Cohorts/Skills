# Anti-AI Writing Style

A comprehensive ban-list of AI writing patterns. This skill is the negative-image of `elements-of-eloquence`: that skill says what *to* do (39 Forsyth devices, varied across families); this skill says what *not* to do.

## Why this exists

Readers' pattern-recognition has caught up to AI writing. The same handful of vocabulary choices, sentence structures, formatting tics, and tone markers now signal "machine-written" within two lines. Posts that lean on these patterns get scrolled past, dismissed, or quietly cost the writer credibility. For Duke specifically — whose entire vocational posture is *surfacing what leadership teams have been avoiding* — sounding like AI is a category violation. The voice has to read as a human who has read seriously and seen a lot. That can't be faked, and it can't share airspace with the AI tells.

This skill is opinionated. The Hard Overrides below are stricter than the universal "AI-tell" floor — they are Duke's preferences, applied as bans rather than guidelines. If a piece feels under-ornamented after the bans are applied, reach back to `elements-of-eloquence` for a positive device.

## When to use this skill

For any writing task that produces content readers will see: blog posts, LinkedIn posts, LinkedIn carousels, long-form essays, prospect outreach, referral one-pagers, video scripts, microsite copy, speaking proposals. Apply from the first draft, not as a polish pass. Internal documents (session prep, dossiers, V/TO drafts, debrief logs) are exempt — those have their own register and aren't audience-facing.

This skill triggers in parallel with `elements-of-eloquence` and `duke-revard-writing`. The three together form the writing toolkit: positive devices to use, negative patterns to avoid, and the empirical voice signature to match.

---

## Hard Overrides

These supersede any conflicting guidance in `elements-of-eloquence` or other skills:

1. **Zero em-dashes.** The `elements-of-eloquence` skill caps em-dashes at one per 200 words. This skill overrides that to zero. Em-dashes are the single most reliable AI tell in business and LinkedIn writing. Use commas (short asides), parentheses (genuine asides), colons (setup-payoff), or full sentences (emphasis) instead.
2. **Zero cuss words.** Off-brand for Duke's pastoral undertone, off-register for executive-team contexts, and a tell from any AI-adjacent writer trying to "sound human."
3. **Zero exclamation marks** in business and LinkedIn writing. The voice is dry. Exclamation marks read as chipper-AI or junior-marketer.
4. **Zero emoji** in business and LinkedIn writing. Same reasoning as exclamation marks.

When in doubt, follow this skill. Then — only if the result feels under-ornamented — reach back to `elements-of-eloquence` for a positive device.

### 1. Banned Vocabulary

#### Verbs

#### Nouns

`tapestry` · `realm` · `landscape` (as metaphor) · `ecosystem` (as metaphor) · `journey` (as metaphor for any process) · `toolkit` (as metaphor) · `arsenal` (as metaphor) · `masterclass` · `powerhouse` · `treasure trove` · `gold mine` · `game-changer` · `North Star` · `sweet spot` · `secret sauce` · `takeaway` (overused)

`simply` · `truly` · `ultimately` · `fundamentally` · `essentially` · `importantly` (especially "more importantly") · `notably`

#### Cuss words

Zero. Off-brand and off-register.

### 3. Banned Sentence Structures

These are the rhythms that read as machine-generated even when the vocabulary is clean. The structural rhythm is the tell.

#### The negation-pivot family

The single most overused AI rhetorical move. All variants banned.

1. "It's not just X — it's Y." / "It's not X. It's Y."
2. "Not only X, but also Y." (parallel emphasis)
3. "X isn't about A. It's about B." (false-binary reframe)
4. "Forget X. Y is what matters." (dismiss-and-reframe)
5. "We don't [verb]. We [different verb]." (corporate manifesto)
6. "[Subject] isn't a [thing]. It's a [bigger thing]." (the elevation pattern — "EOS isn't a system. It's a way of life.")
7. "Yes, X. But also Y." (concession-pivot)
8. "Less of X, more of Y." (compression cliché)
9. "Not just X but Y." (the clean version of #1, also banned)

**Substitute:** Real antithesis varies its structure. Use *was/wasn't, do/don't, then/now, most/few, said/meant*. Or use a chiasmus (Forsyth A-B-B-A) for the closing line: *"You don't get the team you want. You get the team you tolerate."*

#### Rhetorical question, then self-answer

10. "What does this mean? It means…"
11. "Why does this matter? Because…"
12. "Here's why: [answer]."
13. "The reason? [Answer]."

**Substitute:** State the claim directly. The self-answered rhetorical question is throat-clearing.

#### The predictable tricolon (rule of three used as a tic)

14. Three short parallel sentences as closer: *"Show up. Speak up. Stand up."*
15. Three-adjective stacking: *"It's bold, beautiful, and brilliant."*
16. Three-noun lists used reflexively rather than because the content actually has three pieces.

**Substitute:** Tricolons are powerful precisely because they punctuate. Cap at two per LinkedIn post, three per blog post. Make the third item slightly longer or slightly different (the asymmetry is what makes the brain register the pattern as resolved). Mix tricolons with two-part isocolons and single declaratives.

#### Echo-restate patterns

17. Saying the same thing twice in different words for rhythm: *"They were tired. Exhausted, even."*
18. Bookend structure: *"It starts with X. It ends with Y."*
19. Anaphora used predictably: *"We build. We test. We ship."*

**Substitute:** Anaphora itself is fine (it's the highest-leverage Forsyth device for long-form), but stack three or four — not two — and only when building toward a real point. *"This is what your scorecard reveals. This is what your accountability chart demands. This is what most leadership teams refuse to look at."* Three is the floor; predictable two-beat anaphora is the AI tell.

#### Hierarchy-implication patterns

20. *"Some people X. Others Y. The best Z."* (the implicit-superiority taxonomy)
21. *"Most people think X. The truth is Y."* (positions writer as enlightened)

**Substitute:** Stake the position directly without the implicit-flattery taxonomy.

#### Em-dash structural tic

22. Em-dash interruption used to insert a parenthetical reframe. Banned at zero per Hard Overrides above.

#### Listicle structure

23. *"5 reasons why X" / "7 things every leader should know" / "3 lessons from X"* — the BuzzFeed numbered-listicle format reads AI even when human-written.

**Substitute:** A specific number in the *body* of the post, not the headline (Fourteenth Rule). *"37 leadership teams in three years. Same pattern every time."*

#### Pyramid lead

24. Putting a TL;DR or thesis at the very top, then expanding. Reads as AI-tutorial rather than human essay.

**Substitute:** Open in the middle of the action. The thesis emerges through the writing.

### 4. Punctuation Tics

Where AI tells often hide in plain sight.

- **Em-dash:** Zero. Use commas (short asides), parentheses (genuine asides), colons (setup-payoff), full sentences (emphasis).
- **Semicolon:** Avoid. Use a period and a fragment. Fragments punch harder.
- **Colon as dramatic-reveal setup:** *"And then I realized: [thing]." / "The truth is simple: [thing]." / "Here's what I found: [thing]."* The AI-explainer move. Cut. The period earns the same beat without the throat-clearing.
- **Ellipses (…):** Almost never. Three exceptions: (a) genuine trailing-off paired with aposiopesis as a deliberate device, (b) actual elision in a quoted source, (c) a deliberate beat in a piece that has earned the rhythm. Otherwise it reads as either AI-coy or LinkedIn-influencer.
- **Exclamation marks:** Zero per piece for business and LinkedIn writing.
- **Asterisks for emphasis:** Never. \*This is a tell\*.
- **Scare quotes around innocent words:** *"leverage" / "synergy" / "innovate"* — quoting an unloaded word to signal irony or distance is an AI mannerism. If you mean it, say it. If you don't, don't say it.
- **Parenthetical-aside tic:** Three or more parens in a short piece reads as nervously qualifying. One per piece, used for a genuine aside, is the cap.
- **Rhetorical questions at sentence-end:** *"Sound familiar?" / "See where this is going?" / "Make sense?"* — the LinkedIn-influencer hand-raise. Cut.
- **Run-on commas in lists:** *"It was bold, beautiful, brilliant, and unforgettable."* Four-adjective stacks read as machine. Two adjectives plus one noun, period. Or use congeries (deliberate pile-up) once if length is wanted — that's a *device*, used once, not a tic.
- **Hyphenated faux-compound adjectives:** *"leadership-team-defining moments" / "growth-oriented mindset"* — AI compression. Either commit to a phrase or break into clauses.

### 5. Formatting Tics

How a piece looks on screen is half the AI-tell.

- **Bold for inline emphasis:** Zero. If the writing is doing its job, the rhetoric carries the emphasis. Bold is reserved for genuine section headings in long-form pieces only.
- **Italics for emphasis:** Same. Italics for actual titles of works (books, films) — yes. Italics to lean on a word — no.
- **Headers in short pieces:** No headers in any LinkedIn post. No headers in blog posts under ~1,200 words. In a 600-word post, headers read as deck-slide-converted-to-LinkedIn — pure AI.
- **Bullet lists in narrative writing:** Prose punches harder. Use bullets only when content is genuinely list-shaped (5+ truly parallel items, no narrative thread). Rule of thumb: if you can write the same content as a paragraph and lose nothing, the bullets were a crutch.
- **Numbered listicles** (*"5 Reasons Why," "7 Things Every Leader…"*): banned per category 3 structural ban. Even when the content is good, the numbered-listicle visual format reads BuzzFeed and dates the piece immediately.
- **Emoji:** Zero in business and LinkedIn writing.
- **Hashtag soup at LinkedIn post-end:** *#leadership #entrepreneurship #mindset #growth #success* reads as algorithm-bait. Either zero hashtags or three maximum, all content-specific (e.g., `#EOS`, `#Traction`, `#leadershipteam`).
- **"TL;DR" at top of post:** the AI-tutorial format. Cut. If the post is too long for the reader to invest in, shorten it.
- **"Key takeaways" / "In summary" / "What this means for you" boxes at the end:** AI loves to recap. Don't. Close with a chiasmus, paradox, aposiopesis, single declarative, or direct address.
- **Block quotes for emphasis (rather than for actual quotation):** AI uses block-quote formatting to signal "this part is important." Reserved for actual quoted material.
- **Tables in non-data writing:** AI defaults to tables for any "comparison" content. In long-form prose, the table almost always reads as a tell that the writer didn't want to do the prose work. Use tables only when the content is genuinely cells-and-rows (data, schedules, matrices).
- **Title Case in headings inside posts:** When headers are used (long-form only), use sentence case — *"Three uniques and the marketing strategy"* not *"Three Uniques And The Marketing Strategy."*
- **ALL CAPS for emphasis:** Never.
- **Inline backticks in non-code writing:** No. Reserve for actual code, file paths, or technical terms-of-art.
- **The "every-sentence-on-its-own-line" LinkedIn format:** Every sentence its own paragraph with a blank line between. Reads as bot or as someone who took a "LinkedIn growth" course. Vary paragraph density: dense prose blocks alongside one-sentence breath-lines, not uniform breath-lines top to bottom.
- **Even paragraph length overall:** Vary aggressively — a one-sentence paragraph next to a five-sentence paragraph next to a fragment.

### 6. Tone Markers and Sycophancy

Register layer — the *vibe* tells. Duke's voice is dry, expertise-led, pastoral undertone, reserved.

- **Hollow validation:** *"Great question!" / "Excellent point." / "What a thoughtful prompt." / "I love this question." / "That's such an interesting framing." / "You're absolutely right."* Zero.
- **Performative excitement:** *"I'm so excited to dive in!" / "What a great topic!" / "This is such a fun one!" / "Love this."* Cut. The voice doesn't gush.
- **Performative humility:** *"I might be wrong, but…" / "Take this with a grain of salt…" / "I'm no expert, but…"* — when used as a tic rather than as genuine epistemic flag.
- **Empathy theater:** *"I hear you." / "That sounds really challenging." / "I can only imagine how hard that must be."* — when not earned by genuine relational context.
- **Supportive-friend register:** *"You've got this!" / "I believe in you!" / "You're capable of so much!" / "Keep showing up!"* — cheerleader voice. Wrong for every register Duke writes in.
- **Motivational-coach voice:** *"Remember: progress over perfection." / "It's about the journey, not the destination." / "Done is better than perfect."* Cut.
- **Wisdom-broker voice (LinkedIn-philosopher):** *"True success comes from…" / "The real question is…" / "What I've learned over the years is this…" / "Here's what most leaders miss…"* — especially deadly because it's adjacent to Duke's actual voice. Duke's voice surfaces the truth a team has been avoiding; the LinkedIn-sage voice *announces* truth from above. Different posture. The line: does the writing implicate Duke in the room with the team, or float above it.
- **Reflexive agreement / yes-and-amplify:** *"Yes — and what's even better…" / "Exactly! And to add to that…"*
- **"We're in this together" first-person plural:** *"We've all been there." / "We all know that feeling." / "As leaders, we…"* — when there is no actual "we." If writing as Duke, write as Duke. The royal-we / collective-we is AI's way of avoiding a clear narrator.
- **Cheerleader register:** *"Let's go!" / "You're crushing it!" / "Killing the game."* Zero.
- **Reflexive identity-stamp at piece-opening:** *"As an EOS Implementer…" / "As a Professional Implementer…" / "As someone who has worked with X leadership teams…"* — when used as credential-flex rather than because the identity is structurally relevant.
- **Self-disclosure performance:** *"Honestly…" / "If I'm being real…" / "Truth be told…" / "I'll be honest with you…"* — throat-clearing. Implies that everything *not* prefaced was less than honest.
- **Apology preface:** *"I apologize for the confusion." / "Sorry if this comes across as…" / "I don't mean to be blunt, but…"* — the cushion that softens a point until it lands as nothing.
- **Expert-flex:** *"Having worked with hundreds of leadership teams…" / "In my 20+ years of experience…" / "I've seen this pattern in dozens of companies…"* — when used as authority-borrowing rather than as a specific evidentiary anchor. Substitute: The Fourteenth Rule. *"37 leadership teams in three years. Same pattern every time."*
- **"Vulnerable" performance / humblebrag-as-vulnerability:** *"I'll admit something embarrassing…" / "This is hard for me to say, but I built three failed companies before…"* — the LinkedIn-vulnerability performance where the "weakness" being disclosed is actually a credential.
- **"Tough love" mask:** *"I'm going to say what no one else will tell you…" / "Here's the hard truth no one wants to hear…"* — the manufactured-courage opener. If the truth is hard, deliver it without the warning siren.
- **Saccharine encouragement of the reader:** *"You're doing the work." / "This is brave of you to read." / "I see you."* Cut.
- **Reflexive "It depends" / "There's no one-size-fits-all":** the non-answer answer. Fact Finder 9 wiring should commit to a *position* with caveats nested inside, not lead with the caveat.
- **The "X is more art than science" cliché:** the reflexive sophistication-flag.

### 7. Cliché Metaphors and Analogies

Where AI's library of stock images lives. The same handful of figures get pulled across every piece.

- **Journey metaphors:** *"the leadership journey" / "the entrepreneurial journey" / "the EOS journey" / "the journey toward clarity" / "your growth journey" / "the road to X."* Substitute concrete process language (*"the second year of running on EOS," "the quarter after the Focus Day"*).
- **Navigation metaphors:** *"navigating change" / "navigating uncertainty" / "navigating the complexity of X" / "charting a course" / "steering the ship" / "the rudder of the organization" / "course correction."*
- **Light metaphors:** *"shining a light on X" / "illuminating the path" / "the spark of insight" / "a guiding light" / "moment of clarity dawned."*
- **Light-vs-dark moral framing:** *"stepping out of the shadows" / "into the light" / "the dark side of X" / "shadow work"* — when used as filler rather than as a deliberate spiritual register.
- **Building metaphors when generic:** *"building a foundation" / "laying the groundwork" / "the pillars of X" / "the cornerstone of Y" / "constructing a framework"* — when used generically. If the actual content is structural (Six Key Components, Accountability Chart, V/TO), use the *real* structural noun, not the metaphorical building-noun.
- **War metaphors:** *"battle" / "fight" / "warrior" / "trenches" / "in the trenches" / "frontline leaders" / "the battlefield of business" / "ammunition" / "weapons in your toolkit."*
- **Generic sports metaphors:** *"moving the needle" / "knocking it out of the park" / "playing the long game" / "stepping up to the plate" / "in the zone" / "leveling up" / "the game has changed" / "playing chess not checkers."* (Specific autobiographical sports references when the post is genuinely about Duke's identity as a former D-I athlete or his Razorback fandom — fine. Generic sports-flavoring of business writing — dead.)
- **Cooking metaphors:** *"secret sauce" / "the recipe for success" / "ingredients of X" / "cooking up something special" / "marinating on an idea" / "half-baked."*
- **Weather/storm metaphors:** *"weathering the storm" / "the calm before the storm" / "smooth sailing" / "stormy waters" / "rising tide lifts all boats" / "headwinds and tailwinds" / "perfect storm."*
- **Iceberg metaphor:** *"the tip of the iceberg" / "what lies beneath the surface" / "below the waterline."*
- **Mountain/peak metaphors:** *"scaling new heights" / "reaching the summit" / "the climb" / "the view from the top" / "uphill battle."*
- **Plant/garden metaphors:** *"planting seeds" / "cultivating a culture" / "growing roots" / "branching out" / "harvest the fruits of your labor" / "watering what you want to grow."* (Note: "Harvester" the venture is fine because it's a specific brand name, not a metaphor in motion.)
- **Bridge/road metaphors:** *"bridging the gap" / "the bridge between X and Y" / "crossing the bridge when we get to it" / "the road less traveled" / "the path forward" / "the road ahead."*
- **Body metaphors:** *"the heartbeat of the organization" / "the lifeblood of the company" / "the DNA of the team" / "the pulse of X" / "the backbone of the operation" / "the brain of the business."* (The real biological imagery in EOS — *Healthy* as a key word — is fine because it's specific. The generic body-metaphor library is the AI tell.)
- **Tech metaphors applied to humans:** *"rewiring your mindset" / "upgrading your operating system" / "debugging your team" / "your bandwidth" / "circling back" / "ping me" / "let's sync."*
- **Spiritual-flavor metaphors used loosely:** *"the soul of the company" / "the spirit of innovation" / "sacred work"* — when used as decoration rather than as substance.
- **Unlock/unleash family of metaphors:** Already on vocabulary list. Restated as a metaphor family: *"unlock potential" / "unleash growth" / "release the brakes" / "let loose the X."*
- **Layer metaphors:** *"peeling back the layers" / "the next layer of complexity" / "going deeper" / "surface level vs. deep level."*
- **Opening-the-door metaphors:** *"opening doors" / "the door is now open for X" / "this opens up Y" / "closing the door on Z."*
- **The "X is the new Y" formula:** *"Vulnerability is the new strength." / "Slow is the new fast." / "Boring is the new innovative."*
- **The "X eats Y for breakfast" formula:** *"Culture eats strategy for breakfast."* Even when correctly attributed to Drucker, this has been quoted into uselessness.
- **"More than just X" / "beyond X" framing:** *"It's more than just a system." / "Beyond the framework, there's…" / "More than a job — a calling."* (slides into the negation-pivot family from category 3.)
- **Stock visual openers:** *"Imagine you're standing at a crossroads…" / "Picture a chess board…" / "It was the kind of meeting where you could hear a pin drop…"*

### 8. LinkedIn and Business-Writing-Specific Tells

Genre-specific tics that show up in actual deliverables. Some overlap with earlier categories — restated here as the LinkedIn-specific cap.

- **The hook-line-then-blank-line opener:** *"Here's what I learned after running 100 EOS sessions:"* followed by white space and a list. The "tease then deliver" influencer-template. **Substitute:** prolepsis — open with a pronoun before its noun (*"They show up around year seven, the cracks. Quiet at first. Then everywhere."*).
- **The "I [verbed] [number] [thing] and here's what I learned" template:** *"I ran 50 quarterly sessions in 2025 and here's what surprised me."* Even when the number is real, the template is the tell. **Substitute:** lead with the *finding*, not the credential-aggregation.
- **The "story-arc with reveal" LinkedIn template:** Five short paragraphs, each one sentence, building to a "reveal" in the last line that recasts the opening. The format itself is the tell, regardless of content quality.
- **The carousel-cover formula:** *"[Number] [things] every [role] needs to know about [topic]"* — the listicle structure restated for carousels. **Substitute:** a paradox, a specific observation, or a single declarative on the cover.
- **"3 takeaways from [event/book/conversation]":** the reflexive synthesis-flex. Cut, unless the takeaways themselves are non-obvious enough to carry the post on their own (in which case lose the *"3 takeaways from"* framing).
- **"What [famous person] taught me about [topic]":** *"What Saban taught me about clarity."* Even when the connection is real, the format is dead. Write the lesson, not the format-frame.
- **The "expectation vs. reality" two-column template:** *"Most people think X. Actually, Y."* (negation-pivot family from category 3.)
- **The contrarian-flag opener:** *"Hot take." / "Unpopular opinion." / "I'm going to ruffle feathers here."* If the take is genuinely contrarian, the take's content carries it.
- **The "POV: you're a [role]…" opener:** TikTok-imported format. Dead in business writing.
- **The "if you're a [role], read this" gate-keep opener:** *"If you're a CEO of a company under $10M ARR, you need to hear this."* The post earns its readers; it doesn't credential them at the door.
- **Generic CTAs:** *"What's your take?" / "Drop a comment." / "Curious to hear your thoughts." / "Tag someone who needs to see this." / "Share if you agree."* Either no CTA or a *specific* one tied to the post's actual content.
- **"Save this post" / "Bookmark this":** algorithm-bait.
- **The "PS:" / "P.S." add-on at end of post:** the email-marketer move applied to LinkedIn.
- **Repeating the headline as the closing line:** the radio-DJ "and that's the news at 9" structural cliché.
- **Numbered lists where the items are 90% redundant:** *"1. Be clear. 2. Be focused. 3. Be specific. 4. Be intentional. 5. Be deliberate."* AI's failure mode — five bullets all saying the same thing dressed differently. If a list has fewer than 3 truly distinct items, kill the list and write a sentence.
- **"Carousel" / "Swipe →" as content rather than navigation:** Putting *"Swipe to see more →"* as a slide rather than as the inherent affordance of the format. Carousels should earn the swipe with content, not beg for it.
- **Slide-by-slide identical structure:** Every slide structured `Bold Header / 2-line explanation / Bullet point / Bullet point / Bullet point.` AI's carousel default. Vary per-slide structure: some text-only, some single-sentence, some image-led, some asymmetric.
- **"Steal my system" / "My exact framework" / "Free template below":** the growth-hack-influencer opener.
- **"Founders, this is for you." / "Operators, listen up." / "CEOs, take note.":** the audience-targeting hand-raise. The targeting should happen through the substance.
- **"Quick rant." / "Quick story." / "Quick thought.":** the throat-clearing minimization that signals the writer doesn't trust the post to hold attention on its own.
- **Engagement-bait questions disguised as content:** *"Agree or disagree?" / "Am I missing something?" / "Tell me I'm wrong."*
- **Self-deprecating false-humility opener:** *"I used to think I had it all figured out. I was wrong."* The LinkedIn-influencer redemption-arc template.
- **The "3 years ago I was [low status]. Today I'm [high status]. Here's what I learned." template:** the timeline-flex format.
- **Closing with "More on this soon.":** the cliffhanger-bait.
- **Hashtags inside the body of the post:** *"Building a culture of #accountability and #ownership."* Hashtags belong at the bottom of the post if at all (per category 5 cap).
- **"Tagging" performance:** *"Big shoutout to @PersonA, @PersonB, @PersonC for inspiring this thinking."* — when not a genuine credit.
- **The em-dash-as-LinkedIn-punctuation tic:** Already capped at zero per Hard Overrides. The single most common LinkedIn-AI tell.
- **Excessive line breaks (one-line-per-thought) for entire post:** see category 5.
- **"Bookmarked this for later" type meta-commentary inside posts:** *"I know you'll bookmark this and never read it again — so let me make it count."*
- **Overly-rounded numbers:** *"I've worked with 100s of leadership teams."* Either cite a specific number (Fourteenth Rule) or restructure the sentence to not need the number.

## Cross-Reference: AI Tell → Forsyth Substitute

A working table of the most common AI tells and the Forsyth device from `elements-of-eloquence` that replaces each. Reach for these when a piece feels under-ornamented after the bans are applied.

| AI Tell | Forsyth Substitute |
|---|---|
| "It's not just X — it's Y" | Antithesis with varied structure (was/wasn't, then/now), or chiasmus for the closing line |
| "Here's the thing…" opener | Prolepsis (pronoun before its noun): *"They show up around year seven, the cracks."* |
| Em-dash for every aside | Comma for short asides; parens for genuine asides; period + fragment for emphasis |
| Tricolon in every paragraph | Mix tricolons with isocolons (two-part) and single declaratives |
| Bold for inline emphasis | The rhetoric of the sentence carries the emphasis |
| "And that's why…" closer | Chiasmus, paradox, aposiopesis (trailing off), or single declarative |
| Generic CTA | No CTA, or one specific to the piece |
| "Have you ever…" opener | Flat declarative or specific number (Fourteenth Rule) |
| "In a world where…" framing | Open in the middle of the action |
| Vague "hundreds of leadership teams" | Fourteenth Rule: specific number with specific observation |
| Stacked three-adjective lists | Two adjectives + one noun, period; or congeries used once |
| "Most people think X. The truth is Y." | Stake the position directly without the implicit-flattery taxonomy |
| Self-answered rhetorical question | State the claim directly |
| Listicle headline ("5 Reasons Why…") | Specific number in the body of the post (Fourteenth Rule), not the headline |
| Echo-restate ("They were tired. Exhausted, even.") | Anaphora with three or four beats, not two |
| "Journey" as universal metaphor | Concrete process noun (*"the second year on EOS," "the quarter after Focus Day"*) |

## Relationship to Other Skills

This skill is one leg of a three-skill writing toolkit:

- **`elements-of-eloquence`** — the *positive* reference. 39 Forsyth devices to use, organized by family, with worked examples. Says what *to* do.
- **`anti-ai-writing-style`** (this skill) — the *negative* reference. Comprehensive ban-list of AI patterns, with substitutes. Says what *not* to do.
- **`duke-revard-writing`** — the *empirical* reference. Duke's positive voice signature with quoted exemplars from his actual published writing. Says what Duke's voice actually sounds like.

When writing, all three apply in parallel. Where this skill disagrees with `elements-of-eloquence`, this skill's stricter floor wins (Hard Overrides). Where this skill is silent on positive technique, reach for `elements-of-eloquence`. When the question is "does this sound like Duke wrote it," consult `duke-revard-writing` for the empirical voice match.

If a piece feels under-ornamented after the bans are applied, reach back to `elements-of-eloquence` for a positive device — but only one device per paragraph. The Duke voice is dry, not ornate.
